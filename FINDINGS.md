# Findings: Static or shifting? Stress-testing time-varying flow–ecology models

Working notes for the 12 November 2026 Data Science PWT talk. All numbers come from the scripts in `R/`;
tables are in `output/`, figures in `output/figures/`.

## 1. Replication of Saffarinia et al. (2026)

The authors' Zenodo code runs unmodified against their Dryad data. The only change is in the runner: the
scripts read `z6_states_age1+.csv`, and Dryad names that file `z6_states_age1_.csv`. That file matches
the states regenerated by script 1 to within 1e-14.

| Quantity | Rerun vs published |
|---|---|
| MARSS flow coefficients (C), 12 of 12 | identical (max abs diff 1e-11) |
| DLM time-averaged slopes, 12 of 12 | match to 3–4 significant digits (max abs diff 2.6e-5; BFGS) |
| AICc table (Table 1) | same model ranking, but absolute AICc differs (e.g., age-1+ best model 239.8 vs 248.8) |

Things noticed while replicating:

- **The model-agreement figure.** The paper says MARSS and DLM agree in sign for "about 30%" of cases.
  In the published coefficient file, they agree for **6 of 12 (50%)**.
- **The DLM fits the MARSS states exactly.** The DLM observation variance R is about 0 (1e-9 to 1e-14) in
  every model. All variation not explained by flow goes into the random-walk intercept.
- **The "static" DLM slopes have spuriously tiny SEs.** 9 of 12 DLM slopes have process variance q of
  1e-11 to 1e-16, so the slope is a constant. Their SEs are 1e-7 to 1e-5 because MARSS treats x0 as known
  when reporting state SEs. The narrow DLM intervals in Fig. 6 for these groups are a numerical artifact,
  not real precision.
- **MARSS and DLM estimate different things.** MARSS puts flow in the process equation (flow changes the
  *growth* of log CPUE). The DLM regresses the *level* of the stage-1 smoothed states on flow. Those
  states come from a model with no flow term. So "agreement" between the two isn't a like-for-like test.
  For age-0 channel midwater, the DLM slope is 0.47× the MARSS C.
- The paper says R was estimated "for each region and gear type". The code estimates it by gear only
  (two values). U is fixed at 0, as stated in the paper.

## 2. Simulation design

- **Truth:** random-walk log-CPUE states x_t = x_{t-1} + β_t·flow_t + w_t for the 4 habitat states
  (channel/shoal × midwater/otter trawl). `flow_t` is the real z-scored Jan–Jun Delta outflow, 1980–2020.
- **Observations:** 48 station × gear series with the real age-0 missing-data pattern (55% observed).
  Process and observation variances are taken from the authors' fitted age-0 model.
- **β_t scenarios:**
  - Null: β = 0
  - Constant: β = b
  - Linear decline: b → 0
  - Step at 1987: b → 0
  - Step at 2002: b → 0
- **Effect sizes:**
  - b = 0.08: about the largest MARSS C in the paper
  - b = 0.24: a strong-signal check
- **Replicates:** 100 per cell; 4 states per replicate.
- **Methods**, each exactly as in the authors' code:
  1. **Paper MARSS:** one static C per state.
  2. **Paper DLM:** two-stage, fit to the stage-1 MARSS states with BFGS.
  3. **Era MARSS** (not in the paper): separate C per state for 1980–86, 1987–2002, and 2003–20. It is
     compared with the static model by AICc.

## 3. Results

Full table: `output/sim_recovery_table.csv`. Below, "DLM/truth" is the mean DLM slope divided by the mean
true β.

| Scenario | b | Mean true β | MARSS C | DLM/truth | DLM flags change | Era flags change (ΔAICc < −2) | Era flags change (calibrated) | DLM 95% CI coverage |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Null | – | 0 | 0.000 | – | 2% | 20% | 7% | 2% |
| Constant | 0.08 | 0.080 | 0.080 | 0.17 | 1% | 10% | 2% | 0% |
| Constant | 0.24 | 0.240 | 0.240 | 0.28 | 1% | 17% | 5% | 0% |
| Decline | 0.08 | 0.040 | 0.050 | 0.15 | 3% | 26% | 15% | 2% |
| Decline | 0.24 | 0.120 | 0.150 | 0.24 | 19% | 92% | 81% | 6% |
| Step 1987 | 0.08 | 0.014 | 0.023 | 0.22 | 6% | 47% | 30% | 5% |
| Step 1987 | 0.24 | 0.041 | 0.072 | 0.38 | 21% | 99% | 98% | 16% |
| Step 2002 | 0.08 | 0.043 | 0.059 | 0.16 | 4% | 32% | 19% | 2% |
| Step 2002 | 0.24 | 0.129 | 0.172 | 0.26 | 25% | 100% | 100% | 11% |

"Calibrated" uses ΔAICc < −6.37, the threshold that flags 5% of 200 null replicates pooled across the
main and level-truth studies (the null is the same process under both truths).

Takeaways:

1. **The paper's DLM mostly can't see time variation, even when it's large.**
   - When the true effect falls from 0.24 to 0, the DLM's slope changes by only 0.012 from start to end.
   - It flags a changing effect in at most a quarter of fits. With the realistic effect size, that drops
     to 1–6%.
   - In real data, flat, weak DLM slopes (9 of 12 in the paper) are therefore not evidence that the
     relationship is stable.
2. **The paper's DLM shrinks flow effects toward zero by 60–85%.** It recovers only 15–38% of the true
   average effect. Section 4 traces this to two modeling choices (fitting smoothed states, and
   regressing the level rather than growth on flow), not the random-walk intercept. So "weak" DLM
   slopes partly reflect how the model is built.
3. **The DLM's confidence intervals are badly overconfident.** Nominal 95% intervals cover the true β in
   0–16% of years (`sim_dlm_coverage.png`). When the slope collapses to static, the SE drops to 1e-6 or
   less and coverage is about 0.
4. **The static MARSS C is reliable when the effect really is constant.** It is unbiased, and its CI
   covers the truth 91% of the time.
   - When the effect changes, C is a weighted average that favors **high-flow, well-sampled years**, not
     an average over years. For example, under a 2002 step C = 0.17, against a true average of 0.13
     (`R/05_followup_checks.R`, `output/followup_marss_weighting.csv`).
   - **Flow:** weighting by squared flow fully explains the 1987 step (0.023 predicted vs 0.023
     estimated). Two years carry about a third of that weight: 1983 (19%) and 2017 (13%).
   - **Sampling:** it doesn't fully explain the decline or the 2002 step. Sampling thins out over time
     (70% of station-years observed in 1980–86, 62% in 1987–2002, 42% after 2002), so recent years count
     for less. Adding sampling to the weights closes most of the gap (e.g., 0.164 vs 0.172 for the
     strong 2002 step), but this is an approximation, not an exact formula.
5. **"MARSS and DLM disagree" doesn't diagnose non-stationarity.**
   - Sign agreement is 63% under the null and 69–100% when the truth changes. Disagreement mostly
     reflects weak signal plus DLM attenuation.
   - The paper's 50% (stated as 30%) agreement can't tell a constant relationship from a changing one.
6. **A simple, pre-specified alternative works better, but only with a calibrated threshold.** The era
   MARSS recovers era means closely (`sim_recovery_b024.png`). The usual ΔAICc < −2 rule, however,
   flags change in **20% of null replicates**.
   - This is not an optimization artifact. Refitting the worst null replicates with a BFGS polish, and
     starting the era model at the static solution, changes ΔAICc by < 0.05
     (`output/followup_null_refits.csv`).
   - The likelihood-ratio statistics really are too large for the textbook χ²₈ reference: 8 of 100
     nulls exceed what χ²₈ puts at 0.4%. With 41 years of random-walk data the large-sample theory
     doesn't hold. AICc's small-sample correction also uses the ~1,000 station-years as the sample size,
     when the effective sample size is much smaller.
   - The fix is to calibrate the threshold on simulated nulls. Pooling 200 null replicates gives
     ΔAICc < −6.37 for a 5% false-positive rate. The threshold is itself uncertain: its bootstrap 90%
     interval is −7.6 to −4.4, and 100 nulls per study gave −7.45 and −3.21
     (`output/followup_era_calibrated_power.csv`).
   - At that threshold the era model still catches strong steps in 98–100% of fits and a strong
     decline in 81%.
   - At the realistic effect size (b = 0.08), power is only 15–30%. With these data, a real shift of
     plausible size would usually go undetected by any of these methods.

## 4. Why the paper's DLM shrinks the flow effect

`R/03_run_simulation.R ... dlm_variants` refits the same simulated data (same seeds as the main study),
changing one modeling choice at a time. Variants B–D use the simulated true states, which are never
observed in practice; they are diagnostics. Variant A reproduces the main study's DLM slopes exactly.
Tables: `output/ext_dlm_variants.csv`; figures: `ext_dlm_variants_b024.png`,
`ext_dlm_variants_ratio.png`.

| Variant | Change from the previous row | Share of true effect recovered | Flags change, strong decline/steps (b = 0.24) | Flags change, null |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A. Paper DLM | – (smoothed stage-1 states, level ~ flow, random-walk intercept) | 0.15–0.43 | 19–25% | 2% |
| B. True states | Fit the true states instead of the smoothed ones | 0.50–0.88 | 51–72% | 11% |
| C. Growth | Regress the year-to-year change, not the level, on flow | 1.0–1.5 | 97–100% | 12% |
| D. No intercept | Drop the random-walk intercept | 1.0–1.5 | 97–100% | 13% |
| E. One-stage MARSS | Time-varying flow effect in the MARSS process equation, fit to station data (usable in practice) | 1.0–1.5 | 72–89% | 16% |

Takeaways:

1. **Two choices each cut the effect roughly in half, so the DLM recovers about a quarter of it.**
   - **Smoothed states:** the stage-1 states come from a model without flow, so smoothing removes part
     of the flow signal before the DLM sees it (A → B).
   - **Level vs growth:** when flow drives growth, regressing the *level* on flow halves the slope
     again (B → C).
   - **The random-walk intercept doesn't matter:** C and D are essentially identical. (I had guessed it
     did; it doesn't.)
2. **A one-stage model with a time-varying flow effect in the process equation fixes most of it.**
   Variant E is fit to the station data, like the paper's MARSS, and recovers the full effect and its
   trajectory (`ext_dlm_variants_b024.png`). It flags strong changes in 72–89% of fits, versus 19–25%
   for the paper's DLM. Ratios above 1 for the 1987 step reflect the same flow weighting as the static C
   (section 3).
3. **Even correctly specified, the slope-variance test flags change in 12–16% of null fits.**
   "q_slope > 0" is not a calibrated test, just like ΔAICc < −2 for the era model.
4. **Overconfident intervals are a MARSS state-SE issue, not just the paper's.** Even variants C–E cover
   the true effect in only 9–13% of years when the true effect is constant. When the slope variance
   collapses to 0, the slope becomes a constant whose SE comes from treating x0 as known. Section 7
   shows a fix: a prior on x0.

## 5. When the DLM's own model is the truth

`R/03_run_simulation.R ... level_truth` repeats the whole design with the DLM's structure as the truth.
Log CPUE is a random walk plus β_t·flow_t, so flow shifts the *level*, not the growth. Same variances,
stations, and missing-data pattern; new seeds. Tables: `output/ext_level_truth.csv`,
`output/ext_level_truth_variants.csv`; figure: `ext_level_truth_variants_b024.png`.

| Method | Share of true effect recovered | Flags change, strong decline/steps (b = 0.24) | Flags change, realistic (b = 0.08) | Flags change, null |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Static MARSS C (paper) | 0.50–0.69 (constant) | – | – | – |
| Paper DLM (A) | 0.30–0.80 | 91–98% | 12–19% | 3% |
| Era MARSS, calibrated | – | 3–42% | 3–9% | 3% |
| DLM on true states (B, diagnostic) | 1.0 (1.5–1.9 for the 1987 step) | 99–100% | 50–69% | 10% |
| One-stage MARSS, time-varying C (E) | 0.50–0.70 (1.1–1.3 for the 1987 step) | 19–42% | 12–18% | 8% |

Takeaways:

1. **The results flip: each method does best when its structural assumption is right.**
   - **Level truth:** the paper's DLM now detects strong changes in 91–98% of fits. The growth-based
     methods (static C, era MARSS, one-stage E) lose most of their power and recover only half to
     two-thirds of the effect.
   - **Growth truth (sections 3–4):** the reverse.
   - Whether flow acts on the *level* or the *growth* of log CPUE matters as much as static vs
     time-varying. The paper's MARSS and DLM make opposite assumptions, so their disagreement on real
     data partly reflects that structural difference.
2. **Even under its own model, the paper's DLM loses 20–70% of the effect to the two-stage design.**
   The same DLM fit to the true states (B) recovers the full effect; fit to the smoothed stage-1 states
   (A) it does not. The stage-1 MARSS has no flow term, so its smoothing treats flow-driven jumps as
   noise.
3. **Its intervals are still overconfident.** Pointwise coverage of the paper's DLM is 2–68%, and only
   2–12% at the realistic effect size.
4. **At the realistic effect size, detection is weak whichever structure is true.** The best usable
   method flags real changes in under a fifth of fits at b = 0.08.

**Implication for the real data:** the level-vs-growth question can be tested directly by fitting the
flow effect both ways and comparing AICc. Section 6 checks that this works on simulated data; section 8
applies it to the real data.

## 6. Can AICc tell whether flow drives growth or level?

Studies `extra_growth` and `extra_level` refit the main and level-truth data (same seeds). Each fit puts
a static flow effect in the process equation (C, growth) and, separately, on the observations (D,
level), plus the era version of each. Both forms have the same number of parameters, so the comparison
is a straight likelihood comparison. Tables: `output/check_structure.csv`; figure: `check_structure.png`.

| Truth | Scenario | Correct structure preferred (ΔAICc > 2) | Wrong structure preferred |
|---|---|---|---|
| Growth | Constant (b = 0.08 / 0.24) | 99% / 100% | 0% / 0% |
| Growth | Decline or steps, b = 0.08 | 48–94% | 1–21% |
| Growth | Decline or steps, b = 0.24 | 85–100% | 0–3% |
| Level | Constant (b = 0.08 / 0.24) | 99% / 100% | 1% / 0% |
| Level | Decline or steps, b = 0.08 | 63–82% | 5–15% |
| Level | Decline or steps, b = 0.24 | 98–100% | 0–1% |
| Either | Null | (no right answer) | growth 30–46%, level 19–33% |

Takeaways:

1. **Yes, when there is a flow effect.** For a constant effect of realistic size, AICc picks the true
   structure in 99% of replicates and the wrong one in at most 1%. With no flow effect, it picks either
   structure about as often, as it should.
2. **The static estimate in the right structure is trustworthy.** Under level truth, the static D's 95%
   intervals cover the true average effect in 88–94% of replicates (65% for the strong 1987 step,
   because of the flow weighting in section 3).
3. **The era test works much better in the level form.** Calibrated on the 200 pooled nulls, the level
   form needs only ΔAICc < −1.61 for 5% false positives (vs −6.37 in the growth form). Under level
   truth it catches strong steps and the strong decline in 96–100% of fits, and realistic ones in
   18–48%.

## 7. Honest intervals for time-varying effects

Same studies. The one-stage time-varying model is fit in the structure matching the truth in two ways:
with x0 estimated (as in sections 4–5), and with a prior on x0. The prior is centred on the stage-1
estimates for abundance and on 0 for the flow effect, with variance 1 on both. The paper's DLM is also
refit with a prior. Table: `output/check_intervals.csv`; figure: `check_intervals.png`.

| Method | 95% interval coverage of the true effect (share of years) |
|---|---|
| One-stage, x0 estimated | 9–76% |
| One-stage, prior on x0 | **82–96%** |
| Paper DLM, prior on x0 | 0–99% (still biased; see section 4) |

Takeaways:

1. **A prior on x0 fixes the intervals.** Treating x0 as an estimated constant makes MARSS report
   state SEs as if x0 were known. With a prior, the SEs include that uncertainty. Coverage rises to
   82–96% under both truths, close to the 95% target. Point estimates barely move (ratios to truth
   within 0.07 of the x0-estimated fits), except for the 1987 step, where the prior moves them up to
   0.17 closer to the truth.
2. **It doesn't rescue the paper's DLM.** Its slopes are still biased by the two-stage design, so wider
   intervals cover the truth only when the bias happens to be small.
3. **"Slope variance > 0" is still not a test for change.** With the prior it fires even more often
   (33–42% of fits under a constant effect). Decide whether an effect changed with a calibrated
   comparison (e.g., the era test), not from the fitted slope variance.

## 8. What the real data say

`R/07_real_data.R` fits every model form to the real station data (the authors' stations, habitat
grouping, and observation-variance structure), for age-0 and age-1+ with same-year Jan–Jun outflow.
Tables: `output/real_data_model_comparison.csv`, `output/real_data_flow_effects.csv`; figure:
`real_data_tv_slopes.png`.

| Model | Age-0 ΔAICc | Age-1+ ΔAICc |
|---|---|---|
| No flow effect | 34.7 | 6.1 |
| Static, growth (C; the paper's MARSS) | 35.6 | 4.4 |
| **Static, level (D)** | **0** | 5.0 |
| Era, growth | 47.0 | 4.5 |
| Era, level | 5.0 | 3.4 |
| Time-varying, growth | 36.6 | 6.6 |
| Time-varying, level | 1.7 | **0** |

Takeaways:

1. **Age-0: flow acts on the level, not the growth, and the effect is positive everywhere.**
   - The level form beats the growth form by 35.6 AICc units. By section 6, a gap of that size almost
     never points to the wrong structure.
   - The growth form, which the paper's MARSS used, is no better than having no flow effect at all
     (ΔAICc +0.9).
   - Static level effects, per SD of Jan–Jun outflow (95% CI): channel midwater 0.105 (0.053–0.158),
     shoal midwater 0.109 (0.055–0.163), shoal otter trawl 0.082 (0.040–0.124), channel otter trawl
     0.049 (−0.005–0.104). An effect of 0.1 means about 11% higher CPUE in a year with 1 SD more
     outflow.
2. **Age-0: no evidence that the flow effect changed over time.**
   - The era level model is 5.0 AICc units *worse* than the static level model, far from the calibrated
     threshold of −1.61.
   - The time-varying level model is 1.7 worse. Its slopes drift gently downward in the shoal habitats
     (the paper's "weakening"), but the 95% bands overlap a constant line throughout
     (`real_data_tv_slopes.png`).
3. **Age-1+: the data can't distinguish the models.** All seven fall within 6.6 AICc units.
   - Growth and level static effects are within 0.7 units of each other.
   - Era level vs static level is −1.6, right at the calibrated threshold: borderline at best.
   - The time-varying level model ranks first, but no calibration exists for that comparison.

**For the talk:** with a structure the data support, age-0 Longfin Smelt show a clear, positive flow
effect in all four habitats, and no detectable change in it since 1980. The paper's "weakening"
conclusion comes from a two-stage DLM whose estimates are attenuated and whose intervals are too
narrow; its time-averaged MARSS used the growth structure, which the age-0 data reject.

## 9. Caveats

- Both truths are idealized. Real flow effects could act on both level and growth, lag across years,
  or differ by habitat.
- Era breakpoints are given to the era model, not estimated, so it is an "oracle" for the step scenarios.
- Calibrated thresholds come from 200 simulated nulls and carry their own uncertainty (section 3).
- The real-data analysis uses the authors' processed station data and covariate as published. The
  lagged-flow age-1+ analysis was not refit.
- The prior on x0 (variance 1) was checked only at that setting.
- The EM algorithm hit its iteration limit in 4% of era fits and 0.3% of static C fits. Those fits are
  included as estimated.
